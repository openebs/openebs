Same name, same bytes

Every cluster carries the name of what it holds, so identical content reads as identical across all three columns — the two volumes and the store. Watch a1f0 appear at offsets 0 and 16 in cycle 1 and then at offset 9 in cycle 2: three places, one object.

Two different things the colours say

On either volume, a cluster carries a band for every snapshot that wrote that offset, newest last — so offset 2 ends up petrol and plum, and offset 9 petrol and slate. The two grids therefore look identical once they are in sync, which is the whole point. In the store, a chunk's bands are the points whose delta uploaded it — the reference set the collector consults. Different questions: what happened at this offset, versus who put this object here.

The interval is a floor, not the RPO

The schedule fires every five minutes, which is the soonest a new point can exist. What the destination can actually recover to is that plus however long the upload and the fetch take — which is why the clock restarts when the snapshot is taken, not when the cycle finishes.

Why the first cycle is different

A snapshot's own layer is its difference against its parent. On a volume that already had snapshots that layer describes only the newest writes — so the very first DR cycle has to read the whole chain instead. Getting it wrong is silent: the point is internally consistent and wrong about what the volume holds.

Checking is not the whole protocol

Here the collector and the cycles take turns. In a real store they overlap — a chunk can be claimed by a cycle at the moment it is being considered for deletion. The design handles that by ordering: a delete intent is raised before the check, and a cycle must place its reference before clearing any intent, so a concurrent claim is always seen. It does not rely on the collector waiting a while first.

An idle cycle costs nothing

Cycle 4 writes nothing. The schedule fires anyway — it cannot know — so the source snapshots, finds no change, and commits no point at all — a copy of the one before it would say nothing new.

Inside one chunk: segments in, one name out

The diagram above treats a chunk as atomic. It is not. The engine reads the snapshot in fixed 64 KiB segments — SPDK's SPDK_BDEV_LARGE_BUF_MAX_SIZE, sixteen of them in flight at once — and the mover buffers a whole chunk before it can name it, because the name is the hash and there is no hash until the last segment lands.
